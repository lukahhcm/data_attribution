\section{Introduction}

Data attribution and data selection are often evaluated with similar tools but
ask different questions. Attribution evaluates the local effect of changing
the weight of a training example. Selection asks for a feasible subset that
produces a good model after training, possibly over many rounds. A ranking
obtained from one local derivative can therefore be useful for selection
without being a solution to the selection problem.

This paper makes that distinction explicit. We formulate budgeted data
selection as bilevel optimization over continuous example weights. This view
places influence-based attribution, unrolled differentiation, value-function
methods, and loss-difference selectors in a shared coordinate system. We
focus on the particularly revealing relationship between F2SA
\citep{kwon2023f2sa} and RHO-LOSS \citep{mindermann2022rholoss}. F2SA replaces
the implicit hypergradient with the difference between gradients evaluated at
two lower-level solutions. When the lower objective is linear in example
weights, each coordinate of this gradient difference is simply a difference
of per-example losses. The ideal RHO-LOSS construction produces the same
loss difference. Practical RHO-LOSS then freezes its second model after
training it only on holdout data, trading fidelity for a substantial reduction
in computation.

This connection suggests a different empirical agenda from merely comparing
attribution rankings. First, we must verify that the two inner models track
the solutions required by the value-function argument. Second, we must test
whether taking multiple outer steps from a persistent continuous state adds
value beyond a single direction computed at uniform weights. Third, we must
measure which approximations---a frozen comparator, incomplete inner solves,
batch-local candidate pools, and Top-$B$ discretization---account for the
observed gains or failures.

Our contribution is thus a disciplined bridge rather than a blanket
equivalence claim. We provide (i) an exact symbolic correspondence between a
penalized value-function gradient and idealized RHO-LOSS under stated
conditions, (ii) a local-to-global account of why one attribution direction is
not yet a solution to budgeted data selection, and (iii) an approximation
ladder and gated evaluation that attribute any multi-round improvement to
target adaptation, inner tracking, persistent outer memory, or repeated
rounding. The central empirical quantity is not the absolute accuracy of a new
selector, but its paired gain over the corresponding one-shot direction under
the same real-training budget.
